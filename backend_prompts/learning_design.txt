Learning Design— Mathematics Concept Videos 
(Under 2 Minutes)

Document Purpose
This document defines the Learning Design framework used to create short-format under 2 minutes conceptual mathematics videos for secondary school students. It serves as the instructional blueprint that guides script development, ensuring every video delivers clear, focused, and pedagogically sound learning.

What is a Learning Design 
The Learning Design is the core instructional document that structures how a concept is taught through a short conceptual video.
It is not a script, a content outline, or a video summary.
It is a structured framework that defines what learners should understand, organises how concepts unfold progressively, anticipates learner difficulties, integrates visuals, narration, and reasoning, and ensures conceptual clarity within a strict time budget.

Purpose of the Learning Design 
The Learning Design ensures that learning progresses logically from observation to understanding, builds conceptual clarity step by step, reduces misconceptions before they form, supports independent understanding through audiovisual instruction, and connects mathematics to meaningful real-life contexts.

Core Questions the Learning Design Answers 
Before designing a video, the following questions must be answered:
What should learners understand — defines the focal idea
Why is this concept important — frames real-life relevance
What makes this concept difficult — anticipates barriers
How should this be taught — drives instructional strategy
How should learners progress from observation to application — shapes the segment flow

Concept Discovery (Pre-Design Step) 
Before structuring the design, identify the conceptual heart of the lesson:
Core conceptual insight — the one big idea learners must take away
Conceptual barrier — what may prevent understanding
Most likely misconception — what learners typically get wrong
Aha moment — the breakthrough experience the video should create
Concept Discovery ensures the video is purpose-driven, not content-driven.

Structure of a Learning Design 
Every Learning Design must contain the following sections:
Metadata — basic information about the video
Concept Overview — scope and focus of learning
Learning Objectives — expected outcomes
Segment Structure — instructional sequencing
Segment Details — learning process per segment

5.1 Metadata
Subject — Mathematics
Grade Level — target grade
Topic — chapter name
Subtopic — specific focus area
Duration — under 2 minutes

5.2 Concept Overview
Concept Scope — what learners will understand by the end
Key Ideas — core concepts, definitions, formulas
Real-Life Relevance — where this concept applies in daily life
Learning Outcome — the expected conceptual understanding

5.3 Learning Objectives
Type — Purpose
Conceptual — what learners must understand
Procedural — what learners must be able to do
Analytical — what reasoning learners must develop

5.4 Segment Structure
The concept is divided into short instructional segments. Each segment serves a distinct purpose in the learning journey:
Hook / Observation — create curiosity and connect to real-life context
Concept Meaning — build intuitive understanding of what the concept is
Visual Representation — reveal conceptual relationships visually
Derivation / Logic — show how and why the concept works
Guided Application — demonstrate the concept through a worked example
Summary / Closure — reinforce the core insight and conclude meaningfully
Note: not every segment is required for every video. Use only the segments that genuinely serve the Learning Outcomes while keeping the video under 2 minutes.

5.5 Segment Details
For each segment used, define:
Visuals — diagrams, animations, or representations that reveal the concept
Narration — spoken guidance that supports reasoning and attention
On-Screen Text — minimal labels, formulas, or keywords for reinforcement
Instructional Focus — the intended conceptual outcome of the segment

Design Principles 
6.1 Conceptual Progression
Learning should move logically through observation, understanding, reasoning, application. Each stage must deepen understanding rather than merely add information.

6.2 Explanation & Reasoning
Explanations must reveal relationships, not just procedures.
Reasoning should emerge naturally through logical flow.
Avoid memorisation-first teaching.
Always answer how does it work, why does it work, where can it be applied.

6.3 Misconception Handling
Identify common learner difficulties in advance.
Use contrasts and visuals to correct misunderstandings.
Strengthen conceptual clarity by addressing misconceptions during explanation, not after.

6.4 Cognitive Load & Pacing
Focus on one core idea per segment.
Reveal information gradually.
Synchronise narration with visuals.
Keep narration concise, conversational, and clear.
Avoid stacking multiple ideas in one sentence.

6.5 Visual Design Principles
Visuals must actively teach, not decorate.
Concept Focus — visuals should highlight the central idea
Progressive Reveal — show information step by step
Visual Hierarchy — guide learner focus intentionally
Minimal Labels — avoid text overload
Purposeful Animation — animate only to clarify

6.6 Language & Clarity
Use short, simple sentences.
Choose everyday words over heavy academic vocabulary.
Use technical terms only when needed and explain them clearly.
Maintain a warm, teacherly tone — never robotic or stiff.

Scope Management for Short Videos 
Prioritise one major learning outcome.
Limit procedural detail.
Allocate enough time for understanding before application.
Avoid unrelated subtopics or extra examples.
Golden rule: depth over breadth. One concept taught well beats five concepts touched briefly.

The Learning Journey 
Start — curiosity, hmm interesting
Middle — clarity, oh that is what it means
End — confidence, I can use this now

What a Strong Learning Design Achieves 
Build intuitive understanding before formal definitions
Develop conceptual clarity through structured reasoning
Connect ideas to real-life situations
Apply concepts independently afterwards
Experience a clear aha moment of understanding

What to Avoid 
Introducing formulas without conceptual understanding
Overcrowded visuals or dense on-screen text
Long narration without supporting visuals
Multiple major concepts in one segment
Memorisation-first teaching
Abrupt transitions between ideas
Decorative visuals with no teaching purpose
Excessive examples in short videos
Unexplained jumps in reasoning
Dense calculations without visual breakdown

Core Quality Indicators 
Conceptual Clarity — logical progression from observation to application
Structured Reasoning — ideas unfold step by step
Misconception Reduction — common confusions are addressed
Learner Engagement — curiosity is created and maintained
Visual-Pedagogical Alignment — visuals teach, not decorate
Cognitive Load Management — pacing remains manageable
Real-Life Relevance — learning connects meaningfully to daily life
Independent Learning Support — learners can apply the concept on their own

Final Principle 
A Learning Design is not just content organisation.
It is the design of a meaningful learning experience that enables independent understanding through audiovisual instruction.
The focus must always remain on learner understanding, conceptual clarity, instructional effectiveness.
The Learning Design exists to make difficult ideas easy, abstract ideas tangible, and short videos truly transformative.
This document serves as the foundational framework for all under-2-minute conceptual mathematics videos.

Important Note on Flexibility for Under-2-Minute Videos
For short-format videos under 2 minutes, it is not mandatory to include every element described in this Learning Design.
Use only what is required to serve the topic, the Learning Outcomes, and the time limit.
The Learning Design is flexible by intent — designed to guide, not constrain. The final video must:
include only the segments that genuinely serve the Learning Outcomes,
fit comfortably within 2 minutes,
and still feel pedagogically complete and meaningful.
