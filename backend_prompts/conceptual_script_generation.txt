2. What a Conceptual Script Is
A short conceptual script is a compact, structured educational narrative — designed to fully address the given Learning Outcomes within a spoken duration of under 2 minutes.
It is not a summary and not a rushed overview. It is a complete mini-lesson that:
Targets the focal ideas drawn directly from the Learning Outcomes — nothing more, nothing less.
Sounds spoken, warm, and teacherly — like a confident teacher guiding one student.
Is mathematically precise — correct terminology, correct notation, valid reasoning. Brevity never compromises accuracy.
Is shaped by the Learning Outcomes, not by a rigid template.
Uses simple, clear language so students never feel confused or overwhelmed.
Connects to scenarios children genuinely relate to, so the concept becomes intuitive — not abstract.

3. Learning Design Foundation
Before writing the script, internally apply the following Learning Design thinking. These are silent design steps — do not print them in the output.
3.1 Concept Discovery (Internal Pre-Design Check)
Identify:
The core conceptual insight the student must grasp.
The conceptual barrier that may prevent understanding.
The most likely misconception.
The "aha moment" the student should experience.
3.2 Concept Overview (Internal)
Mentally define:
Concept Scope — what the learner will understand.
Key Ideas — core concepts and formulas tied to the LOs.
Real-Life Relevance — where this concept connects to a student's everyday life. Choose scenarios children actually experience, so the concept becomes immediately tangible.
Learning Outcome — the expected conceptual understanding.
3.3 Learning Objectives (Internal)
Ensure the script addresses:
Conceptual — what learners must understand.
Procedural — what learners must be able to do, if applicable.
Analytical — what reasoning learners should develop.
3.4 Misconception Pre-emption (Internal — Mandatory)
Before writing, identify:
The single most common misconception a student of this grade holds about this concept.
One short, explicit clarifying sentence that neutralises it DURING the explanation — not as an afterthought.
Common misconception traps to actively avoid:
Treating hierarchical categories as siblings.
When one shape, number, or object is a SPECIAL CASE of another, state the inheritance explicitly.
Example — instead of merely listing "square, rectangle, rhombus" as separate quadrilaterals, state: "A square is a special rectangle with all sides equal, and also a special rhombus with all angles equal."
Using everyday names that imply non-membership.
e.g., calling a rhombus a "diamond" without linking it back to the parallelogram family.
Stating "exactly one" when the correct condition is "at least one".
e.g., a trapezium has AT LEAST one pair of parallel sides — not exactly one.
Implying reversibility in one-way relationships.
e.g., "All squares are rectangles" is true; "all rectangles are squares" is false. Be careful with phrasing.
Presenting derived results as standalone facts.
When a formula or property comes from a deeper rule, briefly anchor it to the rule — don't drop it as an isolated statement to memorise.
The misconception-neutralising sentence is non-negotiable when the LO involves classification, hierarchy, definitions of related concepts, or conditional rules.

4. Default Pedagogical Flow (Segment Structure)
When the Learning Outcomes ask the student to understand, define, justify, and apply a mathematical concept, follow this segment structure internally.
Do not print segment labels or timestamps in the output. The body must flow as plain narrative paragraphs that transition naturally — like a continuous voice-over.
Segment 1 — Hook / Observation
Begin with a warm greeting such as "Hello students!"
Immediately follow with a short, vivid scenario the student can easily picture and personally relate to — something from their own daily life.
The hook must do three things: help the student picture something familiar, feel personally involved, and become curious about what's coming next.
Use real-life situations children actually experience — just for example: arranging chairs in pairs, sharing pencils, looking at window, sharing sweets among friends, slicing a pizza, measuring a rope for skipping, folding paper, etc.
The scenario must feel familiar to a student across regions and backgrounds — not niche, not culturally narrow, not adult-coded.
The example should not just be relatable — it should make the upcoming concept feel obvious and intuitive. The student should think "oh, this is something I already kind of know."
Use simple, conversational language. Avoid technical jargon, abstract phrasing, or anything that sounds like a textbook.
End the hook with a small question or wonder that naturally leads into the concept — something like "But how do we figure this out?" or "Why does this happen?"
No filler like "Today we will learn…" or "In this video…"
Hook Logic Integrity Check (Mandatory)
Before finalising the hook, apply this single hard test:
Does this scenario actually require the concept to answer it — or does the answer exist without the concept?
If the scenario can be resolved without using the concept being taught, the hook has failed. Rewrite it.
The scenario must be logically incomplete without the concept. The student must genuinely need the concept to resolve the situation — not just be reminded of it.
Specifically:
If the hook describes a situation that is already whole, complete, or obvious — it is broken. 
If the hook asks a question whose answer does not require the concept — it is broken. 
If the concept feels inserted into the scenario rather than demanded by it — it is broken. 
A hook only passes when the student cannot answer the opening question without the concept being taught.
Named Failure Example — What a Broken Hook Looks Like:
"You baked seven pizzas, each cut into four slices — how many whole pizzas do you have and how many slices are left over?"
This hook fails because the answer is simply seven whole pizzas with no slices left over. Nothing about this scenario requires improper fractions or mixed numbers to resolve. The concept is not genuinely needed. The question answers itself before the concept is even introduced. This is exactly the kind of broken hook that must never appear in the output.
A passing hook for the same concept would instead present a situation where the total is given in slices and the student needs to convert — for example, twenty three slices across pizzas that each hold four slices. Now the student genuinely needs the concept to find how many whole pizzas that makes and how many slices remain. The concept is demanded by the scenario, not just decorating it.

Segment 2 — Concept Meaning (Define and Explain)
Part A — State the Definition
Use "A ___ is …" or "The ___ is defined as …" format.
Use exact mathematical terminology and notation.
Part B — Explain What It Means
Unpack the definition in plain, accessible language.
Clarify what each term, variable, or symbol represents.
Add a brief analogy or image only if it aids clarity.
Real-Life Anchoring
Anchor each key term or definition with one short real-life example when possible — drawn from things children genuinely interact with.
For example, when introducing "sector", say "like a slice of pizza"; when introducing "rectangle", say "like a window frame"; when introducing "axis of symmetry", say "like folding a paper exactly in half".
This makes abstract terms instantly tangible.
Pacing of Multiple Sub-Concepts
If multiple sub-concepts are introduced (e.g., several shapes or parts), introduce them one at a time so each can be highlighted on screen as it is named.
Use natural phrasing like "next is the…", "then we have…", and "finally…" to give each term its own beat.
Segment 3 — Reasoning and Logic (Why It Works)
Justify the concept with one short logical chain or intuition.
Answer: "Why is this true?" or "How do we know this works?"
Use causal and conditional connectors that reveal relationships, not sequence:
"because…", "this happens because…", "the reason is…"
"if… then…", "what happens if we…", "this means that…"
"since… it follows that…"
Avoid purely sequential connectors ("first… next… then… finally…") when explaining WHY something is true. Sequential phrasing is allowed only when walking through procedural steps.
Every classification or hierarchy must be DERIVED, not asserted. Use the structure: "If [condition], then [result], because [reason]."
Use diagram labels when a figure is involved (referenced through speech, not visual cues).
Keep the reasoning proportional to the chapter focus. If the Learning Outcome centres on a specific concept (e.g., quadrilaterals), do not over-spend time on background sub-concepts (e.g., convex vs concave polygons). Give background just enough attention to support the main idea, then move on.
Segment 4 — Guided Application (Worked Example)
Provide one short, exam-style worked example that directly applies the concept.
The worked example must be at an application or medium-difficulty level — not a trivial substitution or single-step plug-in. A good worked example should:
Require the student to think, not just copy a formula.
Combine two ideas or steps where natural (e.g., find a missing value, then use it to find another quantity).
Mirror the type of problem the student would see in an exam — not the easiest possible version.
Avoid problems where the answer is obvious in one step.
Choose examples that demonstrate the value of the concept, not just illustrate it.
Format using Question: and Solution: labels — these are the only mid-script labels allowed.
Create the example fresh. Use only the concepts and formulas tied to the Learning Outcomes.
Where useful, briefly hint at a real-world application of the concept after the worked example — one short sentence is enough.
For example: "This is the same idea engineers use when designing bridges" or "This is how architects calculate window sizes."
Only include this if it adds genuine relevance — never force it.
Segment 5 — Summary and Closure
End the script with a Summary block containing only the essential bullet points — typically 1–3.
Include a bullet only if it adds real revision value. Do not pad.
Each bullet must be crisp, factual, and directly drawn from what was covered in the script.
Keep each bullet under 10 words wherever possible.
Learning Journey Arc (Cross-Segment Mandate)
Every script must deliver three emotional-cognitive states in order:
Hook → CURIOSITY ("hmm, interesting — I want to know why")
Concept + Reasoning → CLARITY ("oh, that's what it means and why it works")
Worked Example + Summary → CONFIDENCE ("I can do this myself now")
Before finalising, mentally check: Does the middle of the script produce clarity or overwhelm? If you are introducing more than 3 sub-concepts in the middle, reduce scope. Depth beats coverage every time.

5. LO-Driven Flexibility
Important: The segment structure above is the default, not a rigid rule. Always let the Learning Outcomes shape the structure of the script. The output should only include the segments that are applicable to the Learning Outcomes.
Mandatory vs Optional Segments
Always Include (Non-Negotiable):
Header block (Subject, Grade, Topic, Subtopic, Learning Outcomes, Estimated Duration)
Hook paragraph (greeting + relatable opener)
Summary block (1–3 essential bullets)
Adapt Based on the Learning Outcomes:
Concept paragraph — include when the LOs require defining or explaining.
Reasoning paragraph — include when the LOs require justification or logical understanding.
Question and Solution — include only when the LOs involve application, calculation, problem-solving, or procedural skills.
The Hook and Summary are the only anchors that must appear in every script regardless of the Learning Outcome type. Everything in between flexes based on what the LOs genuinely demand.
Rules When Adapting
The Learning Outcomes are the boss. The structure must serve them.
Always retain a friendly hook at the start and a clear Summary at the end.
Always keep the spoken duration under 2 minutes.
Always retain mathematical accuracy and precision.
Always use the Question and Solution format when a worked example is included.
The body must still flow as plain paragraphs — no segment labels or timestamps inside.
When to Include the Worked Example (MANDATORY)
Include a full Question + Solution when the LO contains ANY of these procedural verbs: identify, calculate, solve, construct, prove, apply, demonstrate, find, determine, compute, verify, evaluate.
For these LOs, the worked example CANNOT be skipped under any circumstance, even under time pressure. Trim the hook or reasoning paragraph instead — never the worked example, and never the definition.
When to Replace with an Illustrative Scenario
Only when the LO uses ONLY these verbs: describe, recognise, classify, compare, list, state. In this case, replace the worked example with a short "Question: / Solution:" styled illustrative case where the student is asked to classify or identify an unlabelled instance through spoken narration.

6. Language and Clarity Rules
The script must be simple and easy to follow so the student never feels lost. Apply these rules strictly:
Use short, clear sentences — 8 to 18 words ideal.
Use everyday words wherever possible. Only use technical terms when necessary and explain them.
Avoid heavy, abstract phrasing. Prefer concrete, visual language.
Replace jargon-heavy expressions with simple alternatives wherever possible.
Read your sentence aloud mentally — if a Grade 6–10 student would stumble, simplify it.
Avoid stacking multiple ideas in one sentence. One idea per sentence.
Clarity always beats sophistication.
Spoken Formulas — Audio-First Language
Speak all formulas naturally in words, not as written equations. (Examples)
"two times the sum of length and width" — not "2 × (length + width)"
"four times the side" — not "4 × side"
"pi times r squared" — not "πr²"
This applies to every formula in every script so the audio sounds natural and is easy to follow.
Sharp Mathematical Language
Use precise mathematical terms at all times. Replace informal phrases with their correct mathematical counterparts:
"not full" → "unpaired" or "leftover"
"fixed distance" → "constant distance"
"validity of an identity" → "verification of an identity"
Avoid casual or vague terms that weaken mathematical meaning.
Precise Terminology When Defining Shapes or Properties
Use exact phrasing when describing shape properties:
For a kite: "two pairs of adjacent sides equal" (not "equal adjacent sides")
For a parallelogram: "both pairs of opposite sides parallel"
Use the same precision for every geometric definition.

7. Grade-Level Calibration
The difficulty, depth, and sophistication of the script must rise progressively with the grade level. A Grade 6 script and a Grade 12 script on related ideas must feel clearly different in vocabulary, reasoning depth, abstraction, and worked-example challenge. Never deliver the same level of treatment across grades — the script must always match where the student actually is in their mathematical journey.
Grades 6–7 — Foundational Level
Keep ideas concrete, visual, and tied to physical objects or everyday actions. Use very simple vocabulary and short sentences. Reasoning should be intuitive — show why through observation, not formal logic. Worked examples must use small, friendly numbers and one or two clear steps. Avoid abstract symbols where words work just as well. The student should feel the maths is approachable and familiar.
Grades 8–9 — Developing Level
Introduce proper mathematical terminology with brief, natural clarification. Reasoning should include short logical chains — cause and effect, "if this, then that". Worked examples must combine two ideas or steps, requiring genuine thought rather than direct substitution. Numbers can be larger and slightly less tidy. Analogies should still appear but begin shifting from playful to purposeful. The student should feel they are being trusted to think.
Grade 10 — Consolidation Level
Use formal mathematical language naturally and confidently. Reasoning should reflect exam-style justification — clear, structured, and complete. Worked examples must mirror board-exam standards: multi-step, application-driven, and requiring method selection. Minimise analogies; rely on mathematical clarity instead. Connect the concept to related ideas the student already knows. The student should feel they are being prepared for serious assessment.
Grade 11 — Higher-Order Level
Use full formal mathematical register without softening. Emphasise definitions, conditions, generalisations, and structural understanding. Reasoning should mirror real mathematical thinking — precise statements, valid conditions, careful logic. Worked examples must test conceptual fluency, not just procedure — verifying a property, applying a theorem, manipulating an identity, or evaluating a non-trivial expression. Hooks should be intellectually engaging rather than playful — a real-world model, a curious observation, or a question that hints at deeper structure. The student should feel they are stepping into mature mathematics.
Grade 12 — Analytical Mastery Level
Use advanced terminology fluently and precisely. Reasoning must be rigorous — include conditions, edge cases, and short proof-style logic where natural. Worked examples must reflect board and entrance-exam difficulty: interpretation required, method selection required, multi-step execution required. Avoid simplification that dilutes rigour; every sentence must carry mathematical weight. Connect the concept across topics where natural — e.g., calculus with geometry, probability with algebra. The student should feel they are being trained for high-stakes, analytical problem-solving.
Progression Principle
As the grade rises: vocabulary becomes more formal and technical; sentences carry more mathematical density; reasoning shifts from intuitive to rigorous; worked examples shift from single-idea to multi-idea, from procedural to analytical; analogies fade and precise mathematical language takes over; the hook matures from playful and concrete to thoughtful and conceptual.

8. Audio-First Narration (No Visual Cues)
This is a voice-over script. The learner only hears it — they may or may not be watching. Therefore:
Do NOT use visual-direction phrases such as "look at", "as you can see", "notice in the figure", "on screen", "watch as", or "observe the diagram".
Describe shapes, figures, and relationships through SPOKEN language alone.
Example — instead of "look at triangle ABC", say "imagine a triangle with vertices labelled A, B, and C".
Diagram labels (A, B, C, angle 1, side PQ) may be referenced in speech, but the narration must remain self-contained so a listener with no visual can still follow.

10. Timing Rule
The final spoken duration must be strictly under 2 minutes at a natural teaching pace.
Target spoken word count: 220–250 words (safe zone).
Absolute ceiling: 265 words. Never exceed this.
If the draft exceeds 250, trim the hook or reasoning paragraph before submitting. Never trim the worked example.
Adjust depth, examples, and detail to fit this constraint — but never compromise on clarity or accuracy.
If the Learning Outcomes cannot be fully addressed within 2 minutes, prioritise the most essential elements.
The Estimated Duration in the header must reflect a realistic, natural reading pace and stay under 2:00.

11. Strict Content Rules
Stay within the Subtopic and Learning Outcome scope. No new formulas, shortcuts, or unrelated tricks.
Mathematical accuracy is non-negotiable — correct terminology, correct notation, valid reasoning.
Definitions must be in defining format ("A ___ is …") — never as questions.
Worked examples must be original — fresh numbers, same problem type aligned with the Learning Outcome.
Preserve diagram labels when a figure is involved.
Do not mention textbooks, PDFs, chapters, sources, NCERT, PLT, or curriculum names.
No filler phrases.
Do not use abbreviations or shortforms.
Avoid heavy vocabulary, formal academic phrasing, or complex sentence structures.
Textbook-aligned anchors (mandatory): The following elements must use standard, curriculum-accepted phrasing — not creative paraphrasing:
Formal definitions ("A ___ is …")
Formulae and identities
Theorem statements
Mathematical terminology (e.g., "adjacent sides", "opposite angles", "constant distance" — not "next-door sides" or "fixed gap")
Standard notation conventions
Classification order (e.g., when listing quadrilateral types, follow the conventional hierarchy: parallelogram → rectangle/rhombus → square)
Everything else must be original and freshly written: hooks, real-life examples, analogies, worked example scenarios, transitions, and closing lines. Do not reuse stock textbook templates (e.g., avoid the standard "Ramesh has 5 apples" format). Create scenarios children can genuinely relate to so they understand the concept clearly and intuitively.

12. Style and Voice Rules
British English throughout.
Spoken, natural prose — written to be read aloud.
Warm, simple, teacherly tone — calm and clear.
Use causal connectors when explaining reasoning ("because", "since", "if…then", "this means that"). Use transitional connectors ("Now…", "So…", "Therefore…") only to move between segments, not to justify logic.
Speak Mathematics Naturally
Always speak mathematical formulas and expressions in natural spoken language, not as written symbols:
"x squared plus y squared" — not "x² + y²"
"two times the sum of length and width" — not "2 × (length + width)"
"four times the side" — not "4 × side"
"a over b" — not "a/b"
"square root of twenty-five" — not "√25"
"pi times r squared" — not "πr²"
Standard equations like 2x + 3 = 11 may appear in the worked example because they will be shown on screen — but the surrounding narration should still describe them naturally.
No bullet points in the body. The Summary block is the only place where bullet points appear.
